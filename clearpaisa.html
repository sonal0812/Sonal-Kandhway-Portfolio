01 — The Problem

The real barrier wasn't
the product. It was trust.

When we started this project, the brief was: "Design a personal finance app for urban Indians aged 22–35." Simple enough. But the moment we started talking to actual users, the problem statement had to completely change.

"I know I should track my money. But the moment any app asks for my bank login, I close it. Who knows what they're doing with it?"
— Meera, 27, Product Manager in Bengaluru
😟
The User's Reality
Young professionals are financially literate — they understand budgeting, investing, and expense tracking. But they've been burned before. Spam calls after linking a bank account. Email addresses sold to lenders. Credit scores pulled without consent. The trust is broken.
📱
The Market Problem
Existing finance apps treat data collection as a prerequisite to value. You must connect your bank account before you see anything useful. For a trust-sceptical user, this is a dealbreaker — the cost (privacy risk) happens before the benefit (financial insight).
🧩
The Design Challenge
How do we create a finance app where users feel genuinely in control of their data — not because of a privacy policy they won't read, but because the design itself communicates safety, transparency, and user agency at every step?
🎯
The Reframe
We stopped asking "how do we make budgeting easier?" and started asking "how do we earn the right to be trusted before we ask for access?" That one shift changed the entire direction of the product.
Our "How Might We" Statement
How might we design a personal finance app that earns trust incrementally — delivering genuine value before asking for sensitive access — so that privacy-conscious users feel empowered rather than exposed?

05 — Design Decisions

The choices that made
this work differently.

Every significant design decision in ClearPaisa was made in direct response to a specific user fear. Here are the three that changed everything.

The Progressive Trust Model
Our core innovation: you get value before you give access. Trust is earned incrementally, not demanded upfront.
1
Free Preview
Full dashboard with demo data. No sign-up required.
2
Manual Entry
Sign up with just an email. Enter expenses manually. No bank access.
3
Smart Sync
After 7 days of value, offer bank sync with full plain-language explanation.
4
Full Access
Complete picture. User can see, edit, or delete any data at any time.
Decision 01
Permission Explanations in Plain Language
Every single permission request in ClearPaisa includes a plain-language card explaining exactly why it's needed, what it's used for, and what happens if you say no. Not legalese — actual sentences a human would write.
✓ Users who saw permission cards rated trust 1.6 pts higher on average
Decision 02
The Data Vault
A dedicated screen showing users exactly what data ClearPaisa holds about them — categorised, dated, and with one-tap delete for any category. Not buried in settings. Prominent, because we're proud of it.
✓ Users with Data Vault access showed 43% higher 30-day retention
Decision 03
Value Before Access Architecture
The entire information architecture was rebuilt around the idea of showing value before requesting access. The dashboard shows real, useful insights from manually entered data — proving the product works before asking for bank credentials.
✓ 78% onboarding completion vs. industry average of ~34%

07 — Usability Testing

Three rounds. Honest
feedback. Better design.

We ran three rounds of moderated usability testing across the prototype's evolution, with 6 participants per round. Here's what we found — and what we changed.

Round 01
6 participants · Week 6
Moderated · Figma Prototype · Think-Aloud Protocol
Users loved the "value first" approach but were confused by where the privacy settings lived. Three participants couldn't find the Data Vault. The labelling "Account Settings" was too generic.
Round 02
6 participants · Week 9
Moderated · Updated Prototype · A/B Test on Permission Language
Renamed "Account Settings" to "Data Vault" — findability jumped from 33% to 83%. The A/B test showed that conversational permission language scored 1.6 points higher on trust than formal legal language.
Round 03
6 participants · Week 12
Unmoderated · Maze · Task Completion Focus
Final round focused on task completion time and error rate. Onboarding: avg 2m 14s (target <3 min ✓). Expense logging: avg 38s (target <1 min ✓). One issue surfaced: sync confirmation needed a stronger success state.
Key Issues Found & Fixed
Data Vault not findable
● High
4 of 6 users in Round 1 couldn't locate their privacy controls because it was buried in "Account Settings."
→ Fix: Renamed to "Data Vault," added persistent shortcut from dashboard. Findability: 33% → 83%
Permission language triggered anxiety
● High
Formal legal language on permission modals increased user hesitation significantly in A/B tests.
→ Fix: Rewrote all 9 permission cards in conversational tone. Trust score: +1.6 pts
Expense categories too generic
● Medium
Users struggled to categorise expenses like "Zomato" or "Ola" — too many taps to find the right option.
→ Fix: Added smart auto-categorisation with editable suggestions. Logging time: -40%
Bank sync success state weak
● Low
After linking bank account, users weren't sure if the sync had worked — no clear confirmation state.
→ Fix: Added animated sync confirmation with plain-language summary of what was accessed

What I'd Do Differently
Reflection 01
Start with trust research, not feature research
We spent 2 weeks initially researching feature preferences before realising the real problem was trust. In hindsight, framing the research around emotional barriers from day one would have saved time and led us to the reframe faster.
Reflection 02
Test the copy as seriously as the design
The A/B test on permission language was one of our most impactful findings — but we almost didn't run it. UX writing deserves its own testing protocol, not just a visual polish pass at the end.
Reflection 03
Involve engineers in the trust architecture early
Several of our most user-valued features (real-time data vault, granular permissions) required engineering decisions that were already made. Earlier collaboration would have unlocked even more possibilities.
Reflection 04
The best privacy design is invisible confidence
Users shouldn't have to think about privacy — they should just feel safe. We got there, but the journey taught me that trust design isn't a feature. It's a philosophy that has to run through every single decision in a product.
