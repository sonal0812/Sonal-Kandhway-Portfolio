How I Work

My design process
isn't a flowchart.

It's a set of questions I keep asking until I find an answer worth shipping. Here's the honest version.

Step 01
Listen First, Design Second
Whether it's nurses on 12-hour shifts or first-time investors checking portfolio apps, the real problem is rarely the brief. I start with deep user conversations — clinical contextual inquiry, fintech trust interviews, learner motivation studies — before a single wireframe.
User InterviewsContextual Inquiry
Step 02
Map the Whole System
Healthcare workflows don't fit on one screen. Banking decisions don't happen in isolation. I map full ecosystems first — service blueprints for clinical handoffs, journey maps for lending applications, IA for adaptive learning paths.
Journey MappingIA DiagramsMiro
Step 03
Design with Intention
Every interaction earns its place. HCI principles aren't a checklist for me — they're how I think. Whether it's a 7-tap rule for emergency clinical workflows or a progressive trust model for finance apps, design decisions are deliberate.
FigmaAxure RPDesign Systems
Step 04
Test, Learn, Ship Better
Design isn't done at handoff. I run moderated tests with real users in their real context — clinicians at the bedside, students in their dorms, investors at the kitchen table. Then iterate until outcome data agrees with design intent.
Usability TestingAnalyticsA/B Tests

Where my expertise runs deepest

After 13 years across healthcare, fintech, and EdTech, my strongest work sits at the intersection of complex enterprise systems and the people who use them daily. UX research and synthesis, information architecture, and design systems are where I'm most confident — turning operator workflows that fight back into ones that get out of the way. How I lead teams

Managing designers is about knowing when to step in and when to step back. My approach is rooted in design critique that's specific over kind, mentorship that focuses on judgment over execution, and giving the team enough context to make great decisions without me in the room. I've grown junior designers into mids, hired teams, run quarterly OKR planning, and built design ops to scale practice — but the work I'm proudest of is people.

What I'm exploring right now

AI in design isn't a buzzword for me — it's daily practice. I'm currently integrating Galileo AI for early concept exploration and Claude / ChatGPT for stakeholder communication and design specifications. The goal isn't to replace thinking — it's to compress the time between insight and prototype so the team spends more time on what matters.

How I Work

Senior designer.
Team leader.

The title is Senior UX/UI Manager. That means I'm responsible not just for the work on screen, but for the conditions that produce good work — the process, the people, and the decisions that don't make it into any case study.

Design critique that's specific over kind

I run critiques where "it looks good" is never an acceptable answer. Every piece of feedback is tied to a user need, a heuristic, or a business constraint. Junior designers leave with a clear action, not just a feeling of having been assessed. The goal is to make the work better and the designer sharper.

Working with PMs and engineers, not around them

I bring design into discovery — not just delivery. That means being in the room when requirements are formed, not handed a brief after decisions are made. With developers, I spec at the level they need: interaction states, edge cases, token references. Handoff is a moment, not the relationship.

Mentoring for judgment, not just execution

The most useful thing I can give a junior designer is not a corrected file — it's a framework for making decisions when I'm not available. I focus mentorship on the reasoning behind choices: why this layout over that one, why research before wireframes, why a design system that nobody uses is just a Figma file. Six months in, they're running their own projects. That's the goal.

Stakeholder alignment without design by committee

Presenting to leadership means translating design decisions into business terms — not defending taste. I frame proposals around outcomes: retention, conversion, support ticket reduction, time-on-task. I listen to pushback as data, not as a threat. The goal is getting to the best decision, not winning the meeting.
